## The Call Before Dawn

It was 3:47 AM when the phone rang. On the other end, a trembling voice — a village headman from the northern fringe of the reserve. A tiger had taken a farmer the previous evening, dragging the body deep into the sal forest. Within hours, a tracking team would assemble at the forest gate, and I would join them as a naturalist observer.

Tracking a man-eating tiger is unlike any other wildlife experience. It is not a safari. It is not a photographic expedition. It is a forensic investigation conducted in near-total darkness, where the stakes are measured in human lives.

## Reading the Signs

The first light of dawn revealed the story written on the forest floor. To the untrained eye, the trail was invisible. But to the trackers — men who have spent decades reading the language of the jungle — every bent blade of grass, every displaced pebble, every droplet of dew missing from a leaf told a chapter of the narrative.

Pugmarks are the most obvious sign, but they reveal far more than mere presence. The depth of the imprint indicates the animal's weight and therefore its condition. The spacing between steps reveals its pace — measured strides suggest a relaxed animal, while uneven spacing with deep impressions suggests a stalk. In the case of a man-eater, the pugmarks are often accompanied by drag marks, scavenger tracks, and the unmistakable silence of the forest.

## The Psychology of a Predator

What drives a tiger to become a man-eater? The popular imagination suggests a bloodthirsty monster, but the reality is far more tragic. Almost without exception, man-eating tigers are animals that have been rendered unable to hunt their natural prey — through injury, old age, or territorial displacement.

The famous Chowgarh man-eater, tracked by Jim Corbett in the 1920s, was found to have two old gunshot wounds that had never healed, one shattering her lower jaw. The Rudraprayag man-eater had porcupine quills embedded deep in his paw, causing constant agony. These were not monsters — they were survivors pushed to desperate measures.

## The Ethical Hunt

Modern man-eater management has evolved dramatically since Corbett's era. Today, the emphasis is on capture and relocation where possible, with lethal action taken only as an absolute last resort. Tranquilisation teams stand ready, and conflict mitigation protocols are well-established. The forest department works tirelessly to identify problem individuals early — often before they become man-eaters — by monitoring cattle kills, tracking habitual crop-raiders, and maintaining an extensive camera trap network.

## A Respectful Distance

As the tracking team moved deeper into the forest, a strange thing happened. We found the remains of the kill — a sambar deer, not a human. The tiger had been misidentified as a man-eater; the farmer's body had been found near a separate tiger kill, and the assumption had been made in the panic of the moment. The real culprit was never identified, and the tiger was left undisturbed.

This outcome, anticlimactic as it may seem, is actually the ideal. It means the forest is healthy, the tigers are wild, and the ancient dance between predator and prey continues uninterrupted. For the naturalists and trackers of Corbett, that is the only happy ending worth pursuing.
